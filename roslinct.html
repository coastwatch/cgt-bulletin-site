The vector is the drug now: what the ex vivo-to-in vivo manufacturing shift means for capacity and characterization

The single most useful industry document in this cycle is a trade feature published in partnership with AGC Biologics, and it deserves reading in a CDMO strategy meeting, because it makes the operational consequence of the in vivo shift concrete in a way that company announcements do not. Three changes, each of which lands differently on this sector's business model. First, the product under contract changes identity. In ex vivo work the vector is a manufacturing tool and the deliverable is the patient's engineered cells, which means impurities can be washed out downstream and the contract is priced around a per-patient campaign. In vivo, the vector is the drug: it goes straight into a person with no purification opportunity afterward, so endotoxin, mycoplasma, residual DNA and host cell protein are held to injectable-biologic standards and the regulatory flexibility that the engineered-cell framing allowed disappears. Second, the scale requirement moves by orders of magnitude — from 200-litre to 2,000-litre bioreactors, with dedicated procedures for mixing transfection complexes, downstream steps that frequently need re-optimizing because column and membrane formats do not scale linearly, and equipment layout, transfer systems and process flow needing to be planned six months to a year ahead. That is a capital-planning problem, not a scheduling problem, and it is a different conversation from booking a slot. Third, the producer cell line stops being an efficiency question and becomes a regulatory one: the field's reliance on HEK293T carries the SV40 large T antigen, a well-characterized oncoprotein interfering with p53 and RB signaling, which downstream purification mitigates in ex vivo applications but which in vivo is a live safety concern, since even trace residual LTA DNA or protein may represent a safety concern. What this means for positioning: the demand that in vivo creates is large, lumpy, capital-intensive and quality-intensive — a different business from per-patient cell campaigns, and one where a well-characterized, injectable-grade bulk vector is the sellable unit. It is also the reason the workflow and analytical development work in this cycle — from AGC's own interviews to the PM577a and LB2501 items — keeps converging on vector quality rather than cell quality. The honest caveat is that this is a sponsored feature and the framing serves AGC's commercial interests, though the technical content is attributed to named upstream and analytical development managers and the regulatory statements are general rather than promotional.

A Blood paper (MD Anderson with Sanofi co-authors, published online October 23, 2026) identifies a mechanistic driver of CD19 CAR-T failure in relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma: single-cell RNA sequencing of tumors at progression from CART19 found, in a subset of tumors, clonally expanded and proliferating CART19 cells carrying transcriptional features of dysfunction — the signature of sustained tumor engagement and chronic stimulation. The authors then built a cell-line model from a post-CART19 progression tumor that reproduced the originating patient's response and failure kinetics, and the contrast is the sharpest result in the paper: CART19 alone produced a transient response followed by progression and secondary central nervous system relapse, while CART19 plus pegenzileukin led to complete eradication of tumors. Same CAR-T product, very different survival — and what it means for how lymphoma trials are designed

A retrospective single-centre study of 109 consecutive relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma patients treated with CD19-directed CAR-T between January 2019 and May 2025 reports that depth of response and durability can pull apart by disease location. Depth of response was comparable between nodal and extranodal lesions (minus 62.1% versus 65.6% at the first follow-up; minus 77.9% versus 78.1% at the second), yet median progression-free survival was 306.5 days in nodal disease against 90 days in extranodal disease. In the exploratory organ-level analysis, pleural, hepatobiliary and adrenal involvement carried the strongest adverse associations, with pleural involvement independently associated with PFS and OS. The authors are careful about the limits — organ subgroups were small (n = 7–19), the analysis is exploratory, and the comparable depth of response at the second follow-up may partly reflect differential attrition, with extranodal patients more likely to be lost to follow-up before that point. The CDMO read: this does not change what a CDMO manufactures — it is one product, one process, one slot. What it changes is who looks like a responder. A program whose pooled response rate looks healthy can be delivering much shorter durability in the extranodal and pleural patients that a real-world referral mix over-represents, and manufacturing providers sit close to that conversation, because site and referral mix is where a cohort's composition gets decided. The practical read is that disease distribution is a design variable for lymphoma CAR-T trials, and that a durability signal which does not show up in a pooled response rate is a signal worth designing around rather than a footnote to the response rate.

ARPA-H is funding the end of the cold chain — and for a CDMO that is a bigger deal than it looks

ARPA-H has awarded up to $36.5M to CYBORGEL, a four-year UC Davis-led project testing whether therapeutic cells protected by intracellular polymer gels survive room-temperature storage, with a goal of more than three months of usable shelf life. Led by Cheemeng Tan with Mayo Clinic, the University of Georgia and Case Western Reserve, it builds on earlier work forming water-rich polymer networks inside living cells that halted cell division while maintaining cellular health. Two other teams are funded in parallel: CaseBioscience (up to $7.3M) and Draper ($7.3M), both under ARPA-H's BoSS (BioStabilization Systems) program, whose framing is unusually blunt about why this matters — biologics "must be kept frozen at ultra-cold temperatures in an unbroken cold chain from factory to patient," a dose worth hundreds of thousands of dollars "can be ruined from just one power outage, one shipping delay, or one freezer failure," and the cold chain "adds tens of billions of dollars in cost every year." Why RoslinCT should read this as strategically significant rather than as a curiosity. The cold chain is the structural reason the autologous cell therapy business has been so hard to scale, and it is the reason CDMOs have been able to sell value-added services layered on top of it. If cell therapies can travel at room temperature, three things change. First, site economics invert: the binding constraint on a cell therapy center today is liquid-nitrogen infrastructure rather than clinical capability, so removing it opens community and rural sites that a geographically distributed CDMO strategy has never needed to contemplate. Second, the distribution model changes — fewer centralized infusion centers means fewer shipment lanes and looser batch-slot planning, which is a direct subtraction from the slot utilization that autologous CDMO revenue rests on. Third, and most consequential, ambient stability moves value from the cell to the vector: a room-temperature-stable product is something manufactured in campaign mode rather than per patient, which is a fundamentally different — and lower-margin, lower-barrier — business than one bespoke product per patient. The honest counterpoint is that none of this is demonstrated yet: this is research funding, the award does not establish that three-month shelf life has been achieved, and Tan's stated benefits are prospective. But the direction of travel is a government priority, and RoslinCT's commercial narrative to clients should assume the cold chain becomes less defensible as a moat over the next three years rather than treating it as permanent infrastructure. The durable CDMO position in this world is analytical: stability formulation, comparability and shelf-life characterization, and the release-testing work that a stabilized product still requires — none of which is the same capability as autologous cell manufacturing, but all of which is defensible.
