Nature Biomedical Engineering published a retraction note on October 26, 2026 for “Engineered outer membrane vesicles enhance solid tumour CAR-T cell therapy” — the BROAD-CAR paper from Tongsen Zheng's group at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital and the Heilongjiang Cancer Institute, Harbin, China. What was retracted: the original article (Nature Biomedical Engineering 2026;10(4):660–675, doi 10.1038/s41551-025-01575-6, PMID 41501251) was published online on 7 January 2026. It described BROAD-CAR, a bacterial outer membrane vesicle platform modified to express a high-affinity anti-PD-L1 antibody and to load plasmids encoding the target antigen for CARs: blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, enhancing CAR T-cell antitumour activity in vitro and in vivo, boosting CAR T-cell expansion by ameliorating the tumour microenvironment, modifying solid tumours in situ so that CAR-mediated lysis could reach antigen-heterogeneous and antigen-negative tumours, and inhibiting tumour recurrence and metastasis in breast cancer mouse models. Why the editors retracted it: the note states that the editors retracted the article at the authors' request; that after publication, concerns were raised about the data underlying several of the figures; that the source data for Figures 5 and 6 and for Supplementary Figures S10b, S10c, S10f, S10g, S12a and S14b exhibit unusual numerical patterns in which some blocks of observations appear to be mathematically related to other blocks of measurements that should be independent; that the authors conducted their own review but were unable to fully account for these issues or provide verifiable copies of the original experimental data; and that the editors have therefore lost confidence in the integrity of this research. All authors agree with the retraction. What is not retracted: a separate and unrelated paper — “PD-L1-targeted OMV priming licenses myeloid co-stimulation and chemokine circuits to potentiate CAR-T therapy in advanced-stage solid tumors” (Journal of Nanobiotechnology, June 23, 2026, PMID 42337606, doi 10.1186/s12951-026-04722-6) from Sichuan University's West China Hospital and College of Biomedical Engineering — reports engineered bacterial outer membrane vesicles displaying a PD-L1-targeting single-chain variable fragment, evaluated for dendritic-cell maturation, co-stimulatory capacity, macrophage polarization and phagocytosis in vitro, and for synergy with dose-de-escalated CAR T-cell therapy in syngeneic models with single-cell RNA sequencing of the remodelled tumour microenvironment. It is a different group and a different platform; its reference list does include the retracted BROAD-CAR paper, which is a caveat a reader should carry. The retraction matters to a competitive reader because the underlying claim was technically attractive — in situ antigen decoration to make antigen-negative tumour cells visible to CAR T-cells — and because it lands in the same window in which the field's solid-tumour CAR T-cell problem is being attacked by entirely different means.

The U.S. Treasury Department's Investment Security Office published a proposed rule on October 26, 2026 — Outbound Investment Security Program Regulations, 91 FR 67396, Docket No. TREAS-DO-2026-0595, RIN 1505-AC94, 38 pages, with comments closing December 7, 2026 — that would implement Subtitle C of the Comprehensive Outbound Investment National Security Act. Why it matters to this field: the proposed definition of prohibited technology expressly names cell and gene therapies. It is therefore not a blanket prohibition on cell and gene therapy development or on licensing. The practical scope runs through the transaction rules: a covered national security transaction includes a U.S. person's acquisition of an equity or contingent equity interest in, provision of debt financing to, entry into a joint venture with, or conversion of debt or contingent equity into equity of a covered foreign person that the U.S. person knows engages in prohibited technology (section 851.209). Manufacturing: The Central Pressure Point

Across Endpoints, GlobalData context, and open reporting, manufacturing appears repeatedly as a cause of:

  • approval delays
  • investor concern
  • supply interruptions
  • longer time-to-revenue
  • higher perceived execution risk

The sector increasingly values partners and internal teams that can reduce CMC uncertainty, support comparability, improve transfer reliability, and make commercialization more executable.

What This Means for the Market

  • Cell therapy sentiment is stronger than broad gene therapy sentiment, especially where evidence packages are more robust and commercial pathways look clearer.
  • High-quality assets can still command large deals, but weak or operationally messy stories are punished quickly.
  • Manufacturing capability is becoming a source of strategic differentiation, not just a background service function.
  • China is now both an opportunity and a competitive threat in high-value CGT categories.
  • Commercial scale-up remains hard, even for highly visible approved therapies.

Integrated View

The current CGT market is very much alive, but it has entered a harder, more disciplined phase. Large pharma is still investing, approvals still happen, and differentiated assets still attract premium valuations. At the same time, the industry is increasingly unforgiving of weak evidence, manufacturing problems, and commercialization friction. The companies—and partners—that win next will be the ones that make advanced therapies not just scientifically compelling, but operationally executable.

🌐 Trade feature (in partnership)August 10, 2026
From ex vivo to in vivo: what changes in viral vector manufacturing when the vector itself becomes the drug

A trade feature published in partnership with AGC Biologics sets out how viral vector manufacturing changes when the vector moves from being a manufacturing tool to being the therapeutic product itself — and it is the most directly useful industry-level piece in this cycle for anyone thinking about in vivo capacity. The paradigm shift as described by AGC's own upstream and analytical development managers: in ex vivo therapy, the vector is a manufacturing tool and the final drug product is the patient's modified cells, so impurities can be mitigated downstream through washing before reinfusion. In vivo, what comes off the production line is injected directly into the patient with no opportunity for further purification, which drives far stricter purity requirements and far less regulatory flexibility — endotoxin, mycoplasma, residual DNA and host cell protein analyses must meet the standards applied to any injectable biologic. The regulatory split runs the same way: in Europe the EMA typically classifies ex vivo vectors as starting materials, and in the US the FDA treats them as drug substances while still allowing a risk-based approach because the final product is the engineered cell. The scale difference is the commercially decisive one: ex vivo therapies need lower vector doses, while in vivo therapies demand orders of magnitude more, requiring bioreactor growth from 200 litres to 2,000 litres with every step scaling accordingly, dedicated procedures for mixing transfection complexes, and downstream steps that frequently require re-optimization because column and membrane formats do not scale linearly. Equipment layout, transfer systems and process flow must be planned six months to a year in advance, per the source. And the cell line bottleneck becomes a regulatory one rather than an efficiency one: the field has relied on HEK293T cells, whose SV40 large T antigen enhances vector production but is a well-characterized oncoprotein that interferes with p53 and RB signaling — mitigable downstream in ex vivo applications, but a live safety concern in vivo, where even trace residual LTA DNA or protein may matter. Note that this is a sponsored feature in partnership with a CDMO, so the framing reflects AGC's commercial interests; the technical content is attributed to named staff and the regulatory statements are general.

A retrospective single-centre analysis of 109 consecutive relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma patients treated with CD19-directed CAR-T between January 2019 and May 2025 reports that depth of response and survival can pull apart sharply by disease location. Baseline tumor burden was independently associated with both PFS and overall survival, at a hazard ratio of 1.17 and 1.21 per doubling of tumor burden. In the exploratory organ-level analysis, pleural, hepatobiliary and adrenal involvement showed the strongest associations with adverse outcomes, and pleural involvement remained independently associated with PFS and OS. The authors are explicit about the limits: the organ-specific subgroups were small (n = 7–19), the analysis is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and the comparable depth of response at the second follow-up may partly reflect differential attrition, because extranodal patients were more likely to be lost to follow-up before that timepoint. Why it matters beyond the paper: the same product at the same dose can produce very different survival depending on where the disease sits, and the driver appears to be starting tumor burden rather than the product's ability to shrink disease. That is first a trial-design and patient-selection problem — extranodal and pleural disease are where response rates look normal and durability looks worst — but it is also a signal about where CAR-T value is hardest to realize, and it argues for stratifying by disease distribution rather than reading a single pooled response rate.

The award supports R&D and does not establish that three-month room-temperature shelf life has been achieved, and Tan's stated benefits — lower distribution cost, improved access in remote American communities and overseas — are prospective, not demonstrated. The BoSS program itself is an admission that the cold chain is a policy problem as much as a technical one.

The blood data: grade 3–4 neutropenia peaked at day 4 in 71.9% of patients, with 34.7% still severely neutropenic at day 28, the point at which infectious risk compounds in patients on prophylactic antivirals and antibiotics. The CAR-HEMATOTOX score (baseline counts, ferritin, CRP, disease burden) was calculable in 86 patients, with 36.0% high-risk; it was the only significant multivariable predictor of severe early neutropenia (aOR 4.04) and the strongest predictor of both early and late thrombocytopenia (aOR 8.22 and 4.20 — high-risk patients had 74.2% severe early T-ICAHT vs 25.5% in low-risk).
