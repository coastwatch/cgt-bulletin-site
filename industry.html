Cell and gene therapy remains strategically important, but 2026 news flow shows a market that is maturing under pressure rather than simply expanding. Manufacturing: The Central Pressure Point

Across Endpoints, GlobalData context, and open reporting, manufacturing appears repeatedly as a cause of:

  • approval delays
  • investor concern
  • supply interruptions
  • longer time-to-revenue
  • higher perceived execution risk

The sector increasingly values partners and internal teams that can reduce CMC uncertainty, support comparability, improve transfer reliability, and make commercialization more executable.

What This Means for the Market

  • Cell therapy sentiment is stronger than broad gene therapy sentiment, especially where evidence packages are more robust and commercial pathways look clearer.
  • High-quality assets can still command large deals, but weak or operationally messy stories are punished quickly.
  • Manufacturing capability is becoming a source of strategic differentiation, not just a background service function.
  • China is now both an opportunity and a competitive threat in high-value CGT categories.
  • Commercial scale-up remains hard, even for highly visible approved therapies.

Integrated View

The current CGT market is very much alive, but it has entered a harder, more disciplined phase. Large pharma is still investing, approvals still happen, and differentiated assets still attract premium valuations. At the same time, the industry is increasingly unforgiving of weak evidence, manufacturing problems, and commercialization friction. The companies—and partners—that win next will be the ones that make advanced therapies not just scientifically compelling, but operationally executable.

From ex vivo to in vivo: what changes in viral vector manufacturing when the vector itself becomes the drug

A trade feature published in partnership with AGC Biologics sets out how viral vector manufacturing changes when the vector moves from being a manufacturing tool to being the therapeutic product itself — and it is the most directly useful industry-level piece in this cycle for anyone thinking about in vivo capacity. The paradigm shift as described by AGC's own upstream and analytical development managers: in ex vivo therapy, the vector is a manufacturing tool and the final drug product is the patient's modified cells, so impurities can be mitigated downstream through washing before reinfusion. In vivo, what comes off the production line is injected directly into the patient with no opportunity for further purification, which drives far stricter purity requirements and far less regulatory flexibility — endotoxin, mycoplasma, residual DNA and host cell protein analyses must meet the standards applied to any injectable biologic. The regulatory split runs the same way: in Europe the EMA typically classifies ex vivo vectors as starting materials, and in the US the FDA treats them as drug substances while still allowing a risk-based approach because the final product is the engineered cell. The scale difference is the commercially decisive one: ex vivo therapies need lower vector doses, while in vivo therapies demand orders of magnitude more, requiring bioreactor growth from 200 litres to 2,000 litres with every step scaling accordingly, dedicated procedures for mixing transfection complexes, and downstream steps that frequently require re-optimization because column and membrane formats do not scale linearly. Equipment layout, transfer systems and process flow must be planned six months to a year in advance, per the source. And the cell line bottleneck becomes a regulatory one rather than an efficiency one: the field has relied on HEK293T cells, whose SV40 large T antigen enhances vector production but is a well-characterized oncoprotein that interferes with p53 and RB signaling — mitigable downstream in ex vivo applications, but a live safety concern in vivo, where even trace residual LTA DNA or protein may matter. A retrospective single-centre analysis of 109 consecutive relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma patients treated with CD19-directed CAR-T between January 2019 and May 2025 reports that depth of response and survival can pull apart sharply by disease location. The award supports R&D and does not establish that three-month room-temperature shelf life has been achieved, and Tan's stated benefits — lower distribution cost, improved access in remote American communities and overseas — are prospective, not demonstrated. The BoSS program itself is an admission that the cold chain is a policy problem as much as a technical one.

BMC Cancer: a pre-infusion score predicts post-CAR-T blood count crashes — grade 3–4 neutropenia in 72% by day 4, with 35% still severe at day 28

A retrospective cohort of 96 consecutive patients at Beijing Tongren Hospital (Nov 2022 – Mar 2025, relapsed/refractory B-cell malignancies) maps the depth and duration of hematologic toxicity after CAR-T and identifies a simple pre-infusion risk flag, using the newly formalized ICAHT framework to separate neutropenia (N-ICAHT) from thrombocytopenia (T-ICAHT). Efficacy was consistent with published experience: ORR 78.3% at one month, 65.5% at three months; CRS in 74.0% but grade ≥3 in only 2.1%, ICANS in 10.4% — confirming that in experienced centers the inflammatory toxicities that dominated early CAR-T literature are now largely manageable, leaving hematologic recovery as the dominant safety concern. The blood data: grade 3–4 neutropenia peaked at day 4 in 71.9% of patients, with 34.7% still severely neutropenic at day 28, the point at which infectious risk compounds in patients on prophylactic antivirals and antibiotics. The CAR-HEMATOTOX score (baseline counts, ferritin, CRP, disease burden) was calculable in 86 patients, with 36.0% high-risk; it was the only significant multivariable predictor of severe early neutropenia (aOR 4.04) and the strongest predictor of both early and late thrombocytopenia (aOR 8.22 and 4.20 — high-risk patients had 74.2% severe early T-ICAHT vs 25.5% in low-risk). Separately, bridging therapy between lymphodepletion and infusion predicted late neutropenia (aOR 4.25), and 9 of 10 patients who developed neurotoxicity also had thrombocytopenia — a hypothesis-generating signal the authors frame as possibly pointing to shared endothelial injury. Reassuringly, G-CSF (78.1% of patients) and thrombopoietin receptor agonists (48.9%) were not associated with increased grade ≥3 CRS or neurotoxicity, removing a common practical objection to early aggressive count support. Why it matters for the sector: the study's framing — "the era of treating CAR-T hematotoxicity as an unpredictable hazard is ending" — is a genuine shift in where safety attention sits, and it points at a specific manufacturing-adjacent service: release testing, bridging management, and post-infusion support logistics for prolonged cytopenia. Caveats: single-center retrospective observational design, n=96, wide confidence intervals (early neutropenia CI 1.01–22.17), and no causal claim.
