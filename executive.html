Bottom Line

CGT is still strategically investable, but the market now rewards execution far more than novelty. As of October 24, the cycle's defining feature is that the sector's two fastest-growing narratives — autoimmune CAR-T and off-the-shelf cell therapy — both took material hits: a patient death in BMS's autoimmune zola-cel program, and Roche walking away from the off-the-shelf CAR-T asset in its up-to-$1.5B Poseida deal. It is safety, access, and whether an off-the-shelf modality makes the autologous manufacturing model unnecessary.

Treasury's proposed outbound-investment rule would treat a covered foreign person's development or production of cell and gene therapies as prohibited technology, with notification and prohibition duties reaching U.S. investors and joint ventures in countries of concern

Treasury's proposed outbound-investment rule (91 FR 67396, Docket TREAS-DO-2026-0595, comments closing December 7, 2026) expressly names cell and gene therapies in the definition of prohibited technology: section 851.226(r)(4) reaches “a pharmaceutical compound, including … cell and gene therapies, and monoclonal antibodies or other recombinant therapeutic proteins, as well as their delivery mechanisms.” The scope is narrower than it first appears: that paragraph is the biomanufacturing limb of prohibited technology, running alongside energetic precursors, novel functional materials and biofuels, and the rule separately addresses biomanufacturing equipment. It is not a blanket prohibition on CGT development or licensing. The operative effect runs through covered national security transactions — equity acquisitions, debt financing, joint ventures and debt-to-equity conversions in covered foreign persons engaged in prohibited technology — with countries of concern being China including Hong Kong and Macau, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia and Venezuela under the Maduro regime. Cell and gene therapies do not appear in the separate notifiable-technology list, which is a notification rather than prohibition regime. Publicly traded securities, investment funds and intracompany transfers are excepted. This is a proposed rule, not a final rule.

🌐 Trade feature (in partnership)August 10, 2026
When the vector becomes the drug: the manufacturing shift from ex vivo to in vivo, from AGC Biologics

A trade feature published in partnership with AGC Biologics describes how viral vector manufacturing changes when the vector becomes the drug rather than a manufacturing tool — the most directly useful industry-level treatment of in vivo capacity in this cycle. Three points, all attributed to named AGC personnel: in ex vivo therapy the vector is a tool and the final product is the engineered cell, so impurities can be mitigated downstream; in vivo, the vector is injected directly with no further purification opportunity, so purity requirements are far stricter and regulatory flexibility far lower, with endotoxin, mycoplasma, residual DNA and host cell protein held to injectable-biologic standards. Regulatory treatment follows the same split — the EMA typically classifies ex vivo vectors as starting materials, and the FDA treats them as drug substances while allowing a risk-based approach because the final product is the cell. Scale is the commercial story: ex vivo needs lower vector doses, in vivo needs orders of magnitude more, so bioreactors must scale from 200 to 2,000 litres, transfection-complex mixing needs dedicated procedures, and downstream steps often need re-optimization because column and membrane formats do not scale linearly — with equipment layout and process flow to be planned six months to a year ahead. And the producer cell line changes character: HEK293T reliance carries the SV40 large T antigen, an oncoprotein interfering with p53 and RB, which downstream purification mitigates in ex vivo work but which becomes a live safety concern in vivo, where even trace residual LTA DNA or protein may matter. Caveat: this is a sponsored feature and the framing reflects a CDMO's commercial interests.
